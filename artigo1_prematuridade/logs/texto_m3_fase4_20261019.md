# Methods

## Study design, setting, and population

We conducted a two-stage time-series analysis of daily counts of preterm births in the 26 large urban concentrations defined by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), which comprise 277 municipalities.[ref] The study population comprised singleton live births at 22 0/7 to 42 6/7 weeks of gestation to mothers residing in these concentrations, recorded in the Brazilian Live Birth Information System (SINASC). Births were restricted to a fixed cohort of pregnancies (estimated start dates from July 31, 2011, to March 5, 2024) to avoid fixed-cohort bias.[ref] The main analysis window ran from April 14, 2012, to August 6, 2024 (4,498 days), the period in which every gestational week from 22 to 36 had a complete set of ongoing pregnancies.

The analysis followed a prespecified analysis plan, version-controlled before any outcome analysis (commit 86069e5, October 9, 2026; archived at XXXXXXXXXXXXX). Deviations from the plan were recorded, with their rationale, before the corresponding analyses were run (Supplementary Material). Reporting follows the STROBE and RECORD statements.[ref] [ethics statement]

## Outcome and exposure

The outcome was preterm birth (22 to 36 completed weeks). Extremely (22–27 weeks), very (28–31 weeks), and moderate-to-late (32–36 weeks) preterm births were secondary outcomes.
Daily maximum and minimum temperatures were obtained from the Brazilian Daily Weather Gridded Data (BR-DWGD, version 3.2.4; 0.1° grid) and averaged over each municipality with area weights; daily mean temperature was the average of the maximum and minimum.[ref] The exposure was the daily mean temperature of the core city of each concentration (the most populous municipality in the 2022 Census; Serra in the Vitória concentration), assigned to all municipalities of the concentration.

## Statistical analysis

In the first stage, we fitted, for each concentration, a conditional quasi-Poisson regression with strata defined by year and month, which controls for seasonality and long-term trends.[ref] Strata without events were removed. The model included the logarithm of the number of ongoing pregnancies at 22 to 36 weeks of gestation as an offset, plus the logarithm of a delivery-risk weight (the mean gestational-week-specific risk of delivery among ongoing pregnancies, by concentration) with an estimated coefficient, to account for the seasonality of conceptions and the gestational-week composition of the population at risk.[ref] Day of the week, national holidays, carnival, and movable holidays (Ash Wednesday, Good Friday, and Corpus Christi) were included as covariates. All 26 concentrations met the prespecified inclusion criteria (complete exposure data, valid gestational age and birth weight in at least 90% of live births, at least 1,000 events, and model convergence); the prespecified fallback specification for nonconvergence was not needed.
Temperature was modeled with a distributed lag nonlinear model.[ref] The exposure–response function was a natural cubic spline with internal knots at the 10th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of the concentration-specific daily temperature distribution (2012–2024), and the lag–response function was a natural cubic spline with an intercept and two internal knots equally spaced on the log scale, over lags of 0 to 7 days. The first 7 days of each series served as lag warm-up and were not part of the fitted data. Dispersion was estimated by the Pearson statistic.
In the second stage, the cross-basis of each concentration was reduced to the overall cumulative (lag 0–7) exposure–response curve, and the four reduced coefficients were pooled with a multivariate random-effects meta-regression fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, with the mean and range (99th minus 1st percentile) of the concentration-specific daily temperature as meta-predictors.[ref] We report Cochran Q, I², and Wald tests for the meta-predictors. Concentration-specific curves were obtained as best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs).
The pooled curve was predicted at the mean of the meta-predictors on a common percentile scale (the average of the concentration-specific percentiles). The minimum-risk temperature (MRT) was the minimum of the curve on a 0.1 °C grid between the 1st and 99th percentiles, with a 95% CI from 1,000 parametric bootstrap samples.[ref] Concentration-specific MRTs were obtained in the same way from the BLUP curves, on the percentile grid of each concentration. The primary contrast was the cumulative relative risk (RR) at the 99th percentile vs the MRT, with the 1st percentile as co-primary contrast; contrasts at the 95th, 90th, 10th, and 5th percentiles were secondary. Contrasts are reported in percentiles and in °C.
When a concentration-specific MRT fell at the 99th percentile, the contrast at that percentile equals 1 by construction (conditional on the estimated MRT, whose bootstrap confidence interval is reported) and is shown without a confidence interval. Concentration-specific contrasts at a boundary were handled in the same way. As prespecified sensitivity analyses, relative risks against the median are also reported, together with the minimum searched between the 10th and 90th percentiles (with its bootstrap confidence interval); the handling of degenerate contrasts and of searches that again fell at a boundary (reported with a flag, without further search) was defined post hoc.
Attributable numbers and fractions were computed with the forward perspective, which assigns to the exposure on each day the events occurring over the following 0–7 days, using the BLUP-reduced coefficients of each urban concentration, as in the MCC study.[MCC 2015] The backward perspective, which requires the full first-stage cross-basis coefficients, was computed as a sensitivity analysis.[Gasparrini & Leone 2014] Components were heat and cold (above and below the MRT) and extreme heat and extreme cold (above the 97.5th and below the 2.5th percentiles). Monte Carlo samples for the empirical confidence intervals were drawn with a concentration-specific seed, so that samples were independent across urban concentrations and shared across the total and the heat, cold, extreme-heat, and extreme-cold components within each concentration. Because the reference temperature was the minimum of each estimated exposure–response curve within the 1st–99th percentile range, daily contributions within that range are non-negative by construction, up to the 0.1 °C resolution of the MRT search grid (negative contributions within that range were negligible; Supplementary Methods), and the attributable fraction is expected to be positive (biased upward) even in the absence of an association; for the same reason, and to the same grid resolution, relative risks against the MRT are not lower than 1, and their confidence intervals, like the empirical confidence intervals of the attributable fraction, are conditional on the estimated MRT. Attributable fractions are interpreted only as the burden that would be attributable if the associations were causal.

## Sensitivity analyses

Six prespecified key sensitivity analyses were used to assess robustness: municipal temperature in a municipal case time series, lag 0–14, a natural spline of time (99 degrees of freedom) instead of strata, adjustment for fine particulate matter (PM2.5, lag 0–1), a fixed coefficient of 1 for the delivery-risk weight, and spontaneous preterm birth. A result was considered robust if, in each key sensitivity analysis, the estimate had the same direction as the primary estimate and fell within its 95% confidence interval. Each sensitivity analysis re-estimated its own pooled minimum-risk temperature; because a contrast against the minimum of the same pooled curve cannot be lower than 1, the direction condition was always met, and the criterion reduced to inclusion in the primary confidence interval. The 99th and 1st percentile contrasts were assessed separately. A contrast was deemed not assessable when the pooled minimum-risk temperature of the sensitivity analysis fell at that percentile. These operational rules were defined after the primary results were known.
The remaining prespecified sensitivity analyses (closed list) addressed residual autocorrelation, the exposure metric, the cross-basis, time trend and calendar (including local holidays), the risk set and offset, the outcome definition, and the pooling model (fixed-effects, multilevel, one-stage, and penalized models); exploratory meta-regressions added one concentration characteristic at a time (macroregion, cesarean delivery, prenatal care, and the municipal human development index). All are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

## Software

Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 using the packages dlnm (v2.4.10), gnm (v1.1-5), and mixmeta (v1.2.0), with mgcv (v1.9-3) for the penalized model and tsModel (v0.6-2) for attributable risk; package versions were locked with renv (v1.2.4), and all stochastic procedures used fixed seeds (20261001 for pooled quantities and 20261001 + i for the i-th urban concentration). The analytic dataset (version 4443971b) and code are available at [repository].

# Results

The analysis included 13,509,914 singleton live births and 1,378,030 preterm births (10.2%) in the 26 urban concentrations (277 municipalities) over 4,498 days (Table 1). Dispersion ranged from 0.98 to 1.12 across concentrations.

The pooled cumulative exposure–response curve was close to flat (Figure 1). The pooled MRT was 28.2 °C (93.8th percentile of the pooled axis; 95% CI, 18.8–29.5 °C, which spanned the entire search range). Compared with the MRT, the RR was 1.003 (95% CI, 0.978–1.029) at the 99th percentile (29.5 °C) and 1.020 (95% CI, 0.990–1.051) at the 1st percentile (18.8 °C) (Table 2). Secondary contrasts at the 95th, 90th, 10th, and 5th percentiles ranged from 1.000 to 1.013, and none of the pooled relative risks excluded the null.

Residual heterogeneity was low (Cochran Q = 92.9, 92 df; P = .45; I² = 1.0%), and neither meta-predictor was associated with the curve (Wald P = .19 for the mean and .31 for the range). Concentration-specific MRTs ranged from 23.1 to 32.3 °C; the MRT fell at the 99th percentile in 10 of 26 concentrations (Figure 3, Supplementary Table S2). In these 10 concentrations, the minimum searched between the 10th and 90th percentiles fell at the 90th percentile in 6, and its bootstrap interval was bounded by the search range.

Lag-specific RRs were small in magnitude (Figure 2): at the 1st percentile, the RR was 0.978 (95% CI, 0.964–0.993) at lag 0 and 1.020 (95% CI, 1.009–1.031) at lag 7; at the 99th percentile, it was 1.010 (95% CI, 0.999–1.021) at lag 0 and 1.010 (95% CI, 1.000–1.019) at lag 7.

If the associations were causal, 1.84% (empirical 95% CI, 0.87% to 2.70%) of preterm births would be attributable to nonoptimal temperature (forward perspective), 0.23% to heat and 1.61% to cold; the extreme-heat and extreme-cold components were 0.08% and 0.07%. The backward perspective gave 2.89% (1.58% to 4.05%).
The attributable fraction should not be read as evidence of an association. Its point estimate is biased upward when the reference is the estimated MRT, and its empirical confidence interval does not incorporate the uncertainty of the MRT or the dependence between BLUPs. In this study, none of the pooled relative risks excluded the null, the pooled MRT was imprecisely estimated (its 95% CI spanned the entire search range), and the MRT fell at the 99th percentile in 10 of 26 urban concentrations. In post hoc analyses, a test of the pooled exposure–response curve did not reject a flat curve (Wald χ² = 1.86, 4 df; P = .76), and the observed attributable fraction (1.84%) did not exceed the 97.5th percentile of its distribution under no association in any urban concentration (median, 1.67%; 2.5th–97.5th percentiles, 0.83%–3.00%; no replicate failed).

In the key sensitivity analyses (Table 3, Figure 4), the 99th percentile contrast fell within the primary 95% CI in 6 of 6 analyses and the 1st percentile contrast in 5 of 6; the exception was lag 0–14 (RR at the 1st percentile, 1.057; 95% CI, 1.019–1.098). All six analyses had an interior pooled MRT.
Across the other 32 closed-list sensitivity analyses with a pooled curve, the MRT fell at a grid boundary in 7 (Supplementary Table S1). Where the MRT was interior, RRs ranged from 1.000 to 1.081 at the 99th percentile and from 1.002 to 1.076 at the 1st percentile; the CI excluded 1 only for the 1st percentile with lag 0–21 (1.076; 95% CI, 1.025–1.130) and with minimum temperature as the exposure (1.036; 95% CI, 1.002–1.071). Adding local holidays did not change the estimates: the RR was 1.004 (95% CI, 0.978–1.031) at the 99th percentile and 1.020 (95% CI, 0.991–1.051) at the 1st percentile. In the warm season (November–March), contrasts on the warm-season axis ranged from 1.007 to 1.022, and none excluded 1. The fixed-effects model gave 1.009 (95% CI, 0.985–1.033) at the 99th percentile; the one-stage model gave an MRT of 27.3 °C (95% CI, 22.9–29.5 °C, upper limit at the 99th percentile) and RRs of 1.019 (95% CI, 0.994–1.044) and 1.020 (95% CI, 0.999–1.042); and the penalized model, against a concentration-specific reference, gave 0.998 (95% CI, 0.993–1.002) and 1.014 (95% CI, 0.989–1.041). No exploratory meta-predictor was associated with the curve (P = .29 to .89).
